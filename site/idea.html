Tribunal doesn’t ask “is this fraud?” It asks what each possible response would cost in rupees — approve, challenge, review, decline — and takes the cheapest one. Then it writes down why.

The idea

A score is not a decision.

At a 2% chance of fraud, declining a ₹200 transaction is a bad trade and declining a ₹90,000 transaction is a good one. One threshold cannot express both — but every fraud classifier ships exactly one.

So a system built on a threshold has to answer every hard case the same way: block it. That is why fraud rules have a reputation for costing more than the fraud. Here is what a sensible hand-written rule set does on our held-out data.

−₹156,541

Not “underperforms”. Loses money — worse than approving every single transaction, over 92 days. It catches 81% of fraud by value, and pays for it by declining 3,607 legitimate customers. Nothing in a precision score would have told you that.

Tribunal replaces the threshold with arithmetic. Give it a calibrated probability and an amount, and it computes the expected rupee cost of all four responses and picks the minimum. The middle rungs of that ladder are the whole point: a passcode challenge costs a real customer almost nothing and stops a fraudster who doesn’t have their phone.

How it works

Six checks, one probability, four prices.

Everything below happens before the authorisation returns — in 55 microseconds at the median.

01 · Features

It can only see the past

29 features built by streaming in time order, so a transaction can never see its own future. Anything derived from fraud labels is held back seven days — at authorisation, nobody yet knows whether a charge will be disputed.

02 · Evidence

Checks that write sentences

Velocity, geolocation, spend profile, merchant risk, novelty, time of day. Each returns what it looked at and one line a person can read — not a number folded into a total that nobody can unpick later.

03 · Probability

A number that means what it says

Gradient-boosted trees, isotonically calibrated. No class reweighting: the policy multiplies this by rupees, so it has to be a real probability rather than a ranking that merely sorts well.

04 · Policy

Cheapest action wins

Expected cost of all four responses, and take the minimum — subject to how many analysts actually exist today. Review admission runs on a daily budget with a controller, not an unbounded queue.

05 · Memo

The reason, written down

A case note assembled from the evidence that fired, in the order that mattered — including the checks that stayed silent. “We looked and found nothing” and “we never looked” are different answers.

06 · Audit

A receipt nobody can edit

Each record carries the hash of the one before it. Change a past decision and every hash after it breaks. Card numbers masked; approvals sampled, because auditing what you let through matters too.

There is no language model in this loop

That is a choice, not a gap. This code sits in the authorisation path of a payment: it decides in 55 microseconds where an LLM call is three orders of magnitude slower, and it is reproducible, which is the only thing that makes an audit trail worth having. Merchant names are also attacker-controlled text — feeding them to a model that decides whether to approve the attacker’s payment is a prompt-injection vector with a direct payout.

The language model earns its place where a human would otherwise write prose: the case note for the ~0.1% of transactions that reach an analyst. Off the hot path, with a template fallback, because a memo outage must never affect a payment.

Failures

What went wrong, in public.

A result with no failures in it has not been looked at hard enough. These are the three that cost the most to find.

Deleted on purpose

A feature that had learned the opposite of reality

The card’s own prior fraud rate ranked second by importance, worth 0.30 PR-AUC. Then I checked its direction: cards with the most prior fraud had exactly zero subsequent fraud. In this simulator a card is defrauded once, so “was defrauded” means “is now safe”. In production it means the opposite — a reissued card belonging to a targeted customer.

I deleted it. Under-protecting the people already harmed once is not a trade worth making.

single-feature ROC-AUC 0.102 · cost of removal ₹21,553
Measured, then muzzled

Geolocation is noise here, so it gets no vote

Impossible-travel detection is one of the strongest signals in real card fraud, and it is built. Then it was measured: distance from home scores ROC-AUC 0.494 against the label — a coin flip. The simulator scatters merchant coordinates independently of fraud.

The check still runs and still reports. It is barred from escalating anything, with the measurement recorded in the code beside the flag.

distance ROC-AUC 0.494 · a coin flip is 0.500
Flattered by a bug

18 months of history compressed into 47 seconds

Time features were built by dividing a pandas datetime by 10⁹. Pandas 3 stores microseconds, so every velocity window silently covered the entire dataset and the 24-hour and 7-day counts were identical on every row.

The AUC went up. Nothing alerted, because leaks always flatter you. I found it plotting distributions and noticing two columns were the same.

now pinned by a regression test · 35 tests passing

And where it still fails

38 frauds worth ₹3,770 get through — small, unremarkable, at familiar merchants during normal hours. Catching them at these thresholds would cost more in false declines than the fraud is worth. 365 legitimate customers are challenged or declined, mostly large first-time purchases at unfamiliar merchants. And the rupee totals are not a forecast: they follow from assumed cost parameters and a simulated dataset whose fraud is more separable than the real thing. The ranking of policies survives all 41 settings tested. The totals do not.

Everything here reproduces from a seed.

No notebook, and no number copied by hand. The failures are in the README, not just the wins.
