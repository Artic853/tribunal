The problem

A score is not a decision

Most fraud projects stop at a classifier: a score, a threshold, an AUC. Real risk teams do not have that problem. They have four the classifier never touches.

01

The right threshold depends on the amount

Declining a ₹200 transaction and declining a ₹90,000 transaction at the same score are not the same bet. One cutoff cannot express both.

02

Blocking good customers costs money too

And it never appears in a precision number. A policy can improve recall and destroy the business at the same time.

03

Manual review is a fixed number of people

Not an unbounded bucket. A policy that sends 4% of traffic to a queue that holds 0.1% is not a policy.

04

Somebody will ask why, later

A merchant, a cardholder, a regulator. “The model said 0.83” is not an answer to any of them.

Tribunal touches 5.8× more good customers than a blocking threshold — and inflicts 19% less friction on them.

₹16,844 against ₹20,810. That is the whole argument for the design in one row: it is not choosing whether to intervene, it is choosing how. Most of the extra interventions are a one-time passcode, not a decline — cheap for a real customer, and useless to a fraudster who does not have the phone. Precision falls to 0.74 from 0.94, and that is the correct trade, because precision counts interventions and the business pays in rupees.

Is the complexity justified?

Partly — and here is exactly where

The honest answer on this benchmark: the expected-cost policy beats a plain threshold by 2.5%, and the review queue is never used. The model is accurate enough (PR-AUC 0.968) that almost every transaction is obviously fine or obviously fraud.

That is a fact about the simulator, not about fraud. So the decision layer was measured against a ladder of deliberately weaker models — restricted feature sets, each honestly recalibrated — to find where it starts to matter.

5c26baf1e433ad47 · sensitivity · 41 cost settings

Robustness

How much of this rests on cost numbers I made up?

Every constant in the cost model is an assumption — none measured from a real payments business. So rather than caveat it in a sentence, each parameter was swept across a plausible range while the others hold: 41 settings over 7 parameters, both policies re-run at every point.

The threshold baseline is handicapped in its own favour throughout — its cutoff is tuned on the very window it is scored on. The ranking never flips. The expected-cost policy wins at all 41 settings, by between +0.17% and +3.97%. That floor is the case where a passcode challenge stops only half of fraudsters, which strips away the friction ladder that is most of the advantage.

Failures

Things I found that I did not want to find

This section exists because a result with no failures in it has not been looked at hard enough. Every number below is reproducible from the repository.

01

My second most important feature had learned the opposite of reality

Permutation importance ranked the card’s own prior confirmed-fraud rate second, worth 0.30 PR-AUC. Then I checked its direction: cards in the top three quintiles of prior fraud contain exactly zero subsequent fraud. In this simulator a card is defrauded at most once, so “has been defrauded before” means “is now safe.” In production the opposite holds — that is a reissued card belonging to a targeted customer.

I deleted it. Shipping a model that under-protects the customers who have already been harmed once is not a trade worth 0.021 PR-AUC.

single-feature ROC-AUC 0.102 cost of removal 0.021 PR-AUC and ₹21,553
02

Geolocation is noise here, so it does not get a vote

Impossible-travel detection is one of the strongest signals in real card-present fraud, and it is built. Then it was measured: distance from the cardholder’s home region scores ROC-AUC 0.494 against the label — a coin flip. The simulator draws merchant coordinates from a fixed radius around each customer, independently of fraud.

The check still runs and still reports what it sees. It is barred from escalating a decision, with the measurement recorded in the code beside the flag.

distance ROC-AUC 0.494 median implied speed 4,011 km/h legit vs 3,462 fraud
03

The escalation rules add nothing

Deterministic escalation rules were built on top of the cost model, then measured. On this benchmark they change the outcome by exactly ₹0 — the cost model already reaches the same decisions. They are kept because the ladder above shows they matter at lower model quality, but “we added rules and they helped” would have been a claim, not a finding. The evaluation reports the system with and without them, side by side.

contribution ₹0
04

A dataset defect that would have invalidated everything

The first dataset came from the simulator’s current release. Its parallelised generator walks spend categories sequentially through the simulated calendar instead of sampling per transaction, so every customer transacts in the same category at the same time: gas_transport appeared only in Jan–Apr 2023, travel only in Jun 2024. In the training window, personal_care contained 137 transactions of which 137 were fraud.

Under a temporal split that makes the category field a proxy for “which month is this”, and everything downstream becomes meaningless. The dataset was regenerated from an older branch that does not have the defect — category shares are now flat at ~1/14 in every month.

05

A timestamp bug that made the model look better

Features were built by dividing a pandas datetime by 10⁹. Pandas 3 stores datetimes at microsecond resolution, so that quietly compressed 18 months of history into 47 seconds: every velocity window covered the entire dataset, and the 24-hour and 7-day counts were identical on every row.

The AUC went up. Nothing alerted, because leaks always flatter you. I found it plotting feature distributions and noticing two columns were the same. There is a regression test for it now.

How it works

Six checks, a calibrated probability, and an argmin over four prices

One transaction enters. Everything below happens before the authorisation returns.

features 29 point-in-time features. State is built by streaming in timestamp order, so a transaction can only ever see its own past. Label-derived features are held back seven days, because at authorisation nobody knows yet whether a charge will be disputed.
evidence Six deterministic checks — velocity, geolocation, spend profile, merchant risk, novelty, time of day. Each returns what it looked at, whether it fired, and one sentence a person can read. Not a score folded into a total.
probability Gradient-boosted trees, isotonically calibrated. No class reweighting: the policy multiplies this number by rupees, so it has to be a real probability rather than a ranking.
policy Expected cost of four actions — approve, challenge, hold for review, decline — and take the cheapest, subject to how many analysts exist today.
memo A case note written from the evidence that fired, in the order that mattered. Not reverse-engineered from an attribution plot afterwards.
audit Appended to a hash chain. Card numbers masked. Every intervention logged, plus a sample of approvals — because auditing what you let through matters too.

There is no language model in this loop

That is a choice, not a gap. This code sits in the authorisation path of a payment: it decides in 55 microseconds, where an LLM call is three orders of magnitude slower. It is reproducible, which is the only thing that makes an audit trail worth having. And merchant names are attacker-controlled text — feeding them to a model that decides whether to approve the attacker’s payment is a prompt-injection vector with a direct payout.

The language model earns its place where a human would otherwise be writing prose: the case note for the roughly 0.1% of transactions that reach an analyst. Off the hot path, with a template fallback, because a memo service outage must never affect a payment decision.

And why it cannot be quietly rewritten

Each record carries the SHA-256 of the one before it. Editing or deleting any past decision breaks every hash after it, and verification reports the exact index where the chain first fails.

$ python -c "from tribunal.audit import verify; print(verify(...))" (True, None, 'chain intact across 1,699 records, head e13b21d1d7afb23d')

This is tamper-evident, not tamper-proof — anyone who can rewrite the whole file can recompute the chain. Fixing that needs an external anchor, and publishing the head hash somewhere you do not control is the cheap version. What it does prevent is the failure mode that actually happens: a decision changed in place after someone asks about it.

Limits

Where it still fails

→

38 frauds worth ₹3,770 get through — 3.6% of fraud by count. They are small and unremarkable: inside the cardholder’s normal range, at familiar merchants, during normal hours. No check fires and the model scores them low. Catching them at these thresholds would cost more in false declines than the fraud is worth.

→

365 legitimate customers are challenged or declined. The memos show most are large first-time purchases at unfamiliar merchants — genuinely ambiguous cases.

→

Calibration is under-confident in the middle band. Transactions scored around 0.55 come in at 0.36 observed. Small samples, but it biases the policy toward over-intervening in exactly the ambiguous range.

→

The absolute rupee figures are not a forecast. They follow from assumed cost parameters and a simulated dataset whose fraud is more separable than real fraud. The ranking of policies survives all 41 settings tested; the totals move a lot and should not be quoted as a projection.

Reproducing it

Everything runs from a seed. There is no notebook, and no step where a number was copied by hand.
